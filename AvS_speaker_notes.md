## Slide 4 — Coopetition (the setup)

**On screen:** Diagram: A → competes with ← buys from S. Three numbers: **26%** (Samsung's share of iPhone manufacturing cost), **$6B** (Apple's component purchases from Samsung in 2010), **4%** (Apple's share of Samsung's total revenue). Quote from the case study.

**What to say:**
> "The answer starts with the relationship. Apple and Samsung were rivals — but Samsung was also Apple's biggest supplier. Samsung built about 26 percent of every iPhone. Apple paid Samsung six billion dollars a year — four percent of all of Samsung's revenue. So they could not actually destroy each other. They depended on each other. That dual relationship — *coopetition* — constrains every move that follows."

**Key term:**
- **Coopetition:** competing and cooperating with the *same* firm at the *same* time. It pulls in opposite directions: you want to win the market, but you cannot break the partner you depend on.

## Slide 5 — Timeline → Verdict (Seven Years in Five Beats)

**On screen:** Visual timeline 2007 → 2012, five major events leading to the verdict.

**The five beats — say each one slowly:**
1. **2007** — Steve Jobs unveils the iPhone, vows: *"We're going to patent everything."*
2. **2010 (Galaxy launch)** — The **Samsung Galaxy Tab 10.1** is so close to the **Apple iPad / iPad 2** that consumer-confusion surveys show >50% can't tell them apart. (On phones, Apple's iconic side-by-side exhibit was **iPhone 3GS** vs **Samsung Galaxy S (i9000)**.)
3. **August 2010** — Apple/Samsung *cross-licensing* talks collapse.
4. **15 April 2011** — Apple files suit; Samsung counter-sues a week later.
5. **24 August 2012** — After only 21 hours of jury deliberation: Samsung *willfully* infringed → $1.05B damages.

**What to say:**
> "The dispute did not erupt overnight — it built over seven years, in five beats. Jobs's 2007 promise to patent everything. The 2010 launch of the **Samsung Galaxy Tab 10.1**, so close to Apple's **iPad** that consumers literally could not tell the two tablets apart. Cross-licensing talks fail in August 2010. Apple sues in April 2011. And the verdict — August 24, 2012 — just 21 hours of jury deliberation: Samsung infringed, willfully. One-point-zero-five billion dollars."

**Key terms:**
- **Cross-licensing:** two firms agreeing to use each other's patents — usually with a fee or trade. The standard industry fix when patents overlap.
- **Willful infringement:** the jury believes the copier *knew* about the patents and copied anyway. It matters because U.S. law allows damages to be tripled for willfulness.

# 🔑 THE THREE PRESSURES — Why Apple Sued

Apple did not sue out of anger. **Three economic forces** pushed Apple into court. Slides 6, 7, and 8 each cover one. If you remember nothing else from this deck, remember these three labels:

| # | Pressure | One-line meaning |
|---|----------|------------------|
| 1 | **Patent Hold-Up** | Too many patents, no clean way out — and the standard fix (cross-licensing) had failed. |
| 2 | **Lost Differentiation** | Samsung looked too much like Apple, and Apple's premium was eroding. |
| 3 | **Platform Tipping** | Android was running away with the smartphone OS market — second place might not survive. |

Each slide that follows defends one of these pressures with theory + evidence.

> *"No clean way out"* = no simple, easy, risk-free solution.

## Slide 6 — Pressure #1: Patent Hold-Up

**On screen:** Headline *Patent Hold-Up* with the line *"When no one owns the road, everyone pays a toll."* Stats: ~10K patents per smartphone; **$10B/year** industry-wide on patent litigation by 2011. Quote: *"Apple & Google each spent more on lawsuits than R&D."* Comparison table — Apple's vs Samsung's patent strategy (offensive, defensive, bargaining chip).

**What to say:**
> "Pressure number one — patent hold-up. To ship a single smartphone, you need to use roughly ten thousand patents. No single company owns them all. By 2011, the industry burned ten billion dollars a year fighting about it. Apple and Google each spent more on lawsuits than on R&D. The textbook fix is cross-licensing — but those talks failed between Apple and Samsung in August 2010. With cross-licensing dead, suing was the only lever Apple had left."

**Key terms:**
- **Patent hold-up:** when you need someone else's patent to ship your product and they can demand a steep "toll" — or block you outright. With 10,000 patents per phone, *everyone* is held up by *someone*.
- **Cross-licensing:** "I'll let you use mine, you let me use yours." Saves both sides constant lawsuits.
- **Textbook fix:** plain-English shorthand for "the standard solution any economics textbook recommends."

**Walking the Patent Strategy comparison table:**
> "Quick read of the table — both companies played the same three-move game, but with different cards.
>
> **Offensive.** Apple's offense was *design and UI* — sue Samsung for copying the iPhone's look and feel. Samsung's offense was different: it counter-sued over **wireless patents** — the standards-essential 3G patents you literally cannot ship a phone without. Different weapons, same logic.
>
> **Defensive.** Apple's posture was Jobs's 2007 promise — *patent everything* — build a wall so dense that no rival can move without bumping into it. Samsung did the same thing in parallel: stockpile patents, so when Apple sues, you have ammunition to fire back.
>
> **Bargaining chip.** This is the punchline. Neither side actually wanted these cases to run forever. Apple wanted court wins so it could demand **better licensing terms** from everyone running Android. Samsung wanted its wireless patents recognized as leverage — *'sue us and we'll block your supply chain.'*
>
> So the table is really showing one thing: **patents weren't being used to kill the other side. They were being used to set the price of peace.** That's exactly why we land on the Slide 12 line — *patents are bargaining chips, not weapons.*"

**Short version (if pressed for time):**
> *"Both companies built offense, defense, and leverage — Apple through design patents, Samsung through wireless patents. Same playbook, different cards."*

**Memorize this sound-bite:**
> *"When no one owns the road, everyone pays a toll."*

## Slide 7 — Pressure #2: Lost Differentiation

**On screen:** Two boxes — *Bertrand* (Joseph Bertrand) and *Hotelling* (Harold Hotelling) — and an "Implication" line. Hotelling-style "taste line" diagram showing Apple and Samsung positioning. Killer stat: more than half of consumers couldn't tell a Galaxy Tab from an iPad.

**What to say:**
> "Pressure number two — lost differentiation. Two old economic models tell us why Apple panicked. Bertrand (Joseph Bertrand) says: identical products lead to a price war and zero profit. Hotelling (Harold Hotelling) adds: the closer two firms stand on a customer-preference line, the harder they have to compete. When more than half of consumers thought a Samsung tablet *was* an Apple product, Samsung was standing right next to Apple on that line. Apple's premium — the reason customers paid extra — was eroding. Suing was about restoring distance."

**Key terms (these will sound smart but are simple):**
- **Bertrand competition** (Joseph Bertrand, French mathematician, 1883): firms compete on *price*. If products are identical → price collapses to cost → both make zero profit. Lesson: never let your product look identical to a rival's.
- **Hotelling model** (Harold Hotelling, American economist, 1929): imagine a beach with two ice-cream sellers. The closer they stand to each other, the more they fight for the same customers. The "taste line" is just that beach — but for *customer preferences* (style, brand, features).
- **Differentiation premium:** the extra price customers pay because your product feels *different and better*. Lose the difference → lose the premium.

## Slide 8 — Pressure #3: Platform Tipping

**On screen:** Comparison table: iOS vs Android (closed/open · network effect · switching costs · game type). Chart: U.S. smartphone OS market share 2006 → 2012, ending at **Android 52%, iOS 32%, BlackBerry 10%, Microsoft 4%**.

**What to say:**
> "Pressure number three — and the biggest. This was not really about phones. It was about *platforms*. iOS is closed: only Apple makes iPhones. Android is open: many manufacturers — Samsung, HTC, Motorola — all run it. Both have network effects: more users → more apps → more users. But that means *one* platform can run away with the market. Look at the chart — BlackBerry collapses from 43 to 4 percent. Android goes from zero to 52. The market almost tipped — it ended in a duopoly: iOS and Android. Apple was competing **for** the whole market. Samsung-plus-Android was competing **in** it. If Apple let Android run unchecked, second place might not survive. Suing Samsung was not just about damages — it was about slowing Android down."

**Key terms:**
- **Network effect:** the more people use a product, the more valuable it gets for the next user. (More iPhone users → more iOS apps → makes iPhone better for the next buyer.)
- **Platform tipping:** winner takes (almost) all. Phones almost tipped — instead they settled into a duopoly: iOS and Android.
- **OEM:** Original Equipment Manufacturer — the company that physically builds the device. Samsung, HTC, etc. are all OEMs running Android.
- **Compete *FOR* vs *IN* the market:** *FOR* = trying to be the one platform that wins it all (Apple). *IN* = many firms sharing one platform, competing on devices (Samsung et al. inside Android).

## Slide 9 — The Credibility Ceiling (the pivot)

**On screen:** Steve Jobs quote: *"I will spend my last dying breath… every penny of Apple's $40 billion in the bank… I'm going to destroy Android."* "Strategic Commitment — 3 Criteria" box: Observable ✓ · Understandable ✓ · Credible ⚠ Partial. Small game tree: *cross-license → reject → sue → counter-sue*.

**What to say:**
> "If Apple was so threatened, why did it not just bury Samsung? Because the threat had a ceiling. For a strategic threat to actually work, three things are needed. Observable — yes, lawsuits are public. Understandable — yes, 'copy us, pay a billion.' And credible — and this is where Apple stalls. Apple still needed Samsung's chips, screens, and memory. It could not actually go all-in. The game tree confirms it: cross-licensing rejected, Apple sues, Samsung counter-sues — exactly the equilibrium game theory predicts when the threat is bounded."

**Walking the "3 Criteria" box (use these connecting sentences):**

> "Now let's test Apple's threat against the three textbook conditions for a strategic commitment to actually bite. A threat that fails any one of these is just noise."

> **Observable?** *"Start at the top. Was the move visible to Samsung and to the rest of the market? Absolutely. Apple filed a public lawsuit and asked for sales injunctions in multiple countries. There is no hiding a 1.05 billion-dollar verdict — every rival, every supplier, every regulator saw it. Check."*

> **Understandable?** *"Next — could Samsung read the message? Yes, and the message was brutally simple: copy our design, face a billion-plus in costs. No ambiguity, no fine print. Samsung — and every other Android maker watching — knew exactly what behavior would be punished. Check."*

> **Credible?** *"And here is where it gets interesting. Did Samsung believe Apple would actually follow through? Partially. On the *push-forward* side, sunk costs and reputation lock Apple in — millions already spent on lawyers, public statements made, and at the same time Apple was suing HTC, Motorola, Nokia, Amazon, and Google. Backing down on Samsung would have collapsed every other threat. So Apple was committed to **sue**. But on the *pull-back* side — Apple still bought six billion dollars a year in components from Samsung. It physically could not destroy its own supplier. So Apple was **not** committed to **win the war**. That's the asymmetry — and that's why we mark this one *partial*, not yes."*

> **The takeaway line:** *"Two yeses and a partial — that explains the whole verdict. Apple's threat was real enough to win the case, but not real enough to stop Samsung. The lawsuit was the maximum punch a partner-rival can throw without breaking its own supply chain."*

**Key terms:**
- **Strategic commitment:** an action so big or costly that your rival believes you will really go through with it. For a threat to bite, the rival must believe you mean it.
- **Sunk cost:** money/effort already spent that you can't get back (lawyers, court fees, public statements). Pushes Apple to follow through — "we've paid for the war, backing out wastes everything."
- **Reputation on the line:** if Apple threatens publicly and then backs down, every other rival stops fearing Apple. Apple was also suing HTC, Motorola, Nokia, Amazon, Google — go soft on Samsung and every other threat collapses.
- **Game tree:** a diagram of who moves first, what the other side can do, and how it ends. Used to find the equilibrium path.
- **Trigger strategy / repeated-game reputation:** in repeated interactions you punish bad behavior so the rival learns. Apple suing was the **minimum** trigger — anything weaker would gut (weaken badly) every other threat Apple was making.

**Why only "partial" credible?** Sunk costs and reputation push Apple *to commit* ✓. But Apple still needs Samsung's chips — it physically cannot destroy its own supplier. So Apple was committed enough *to sue*, not committed enough *to win the war*.

> *"Apple was deep enough that backing out was costly — but tied to Samsung tightly enough that going all-in was impossible."*

## Slide 10 — Two Parallel Games

**On screen:** Two payoff matrices side-by-side. **Game 1** — smartphone competition (a prisoner's dilemma). **Game 2** — supply chain (a repeated game with cooperative equilibrium at $6B / $6B). Below: the *cooperation inequality* formula `r ≤ (πC − πNC) / (πD − πC)`.

**What to say:**
> "Now the deeper logic. Apple and Samsung play *two* games at the same time. Game one — smartphone competition — is a prisoner's dilemma: defection looks tempting on any single turn (*meaning: in any one round, cheating the other side looks like the best move — sue, copy, undercut*). Game two — the supply chain — is a repeated game where cooperation pays year after year. The cooperation inequality just says: as long as the long-term reward of cooperating exceeds the one-time gain from cheating, cooperation holds. With six billion dollars a year on the table, it holds easily. That is why the lawsuit never blew up the supply contract."

**Key terms:**
- **Prisoner's dilemma:** classic game where both sides would do better by cooperating, but each is individually tempted to defect — and both end up worse off.
- **Repeated game:** the *same* game played many times. Because you will meet again, cooperation can become rational (you protect your future).
- **Nash equilibrium (NE):** an outcome where neither side wants to deviate, given the other's choice. The $6B/$6B is the NE in Game 2.
- **Cooperation inequality:** in plain English — *"if the long-term reward of cooperating beats the short-term gain from cheating, cooperate."* Don't memorize the formula; memorize that sentence.

**Unpacking `r` in the inequality (if asked):**
- **`r` = your impatience** (how much you devalue future money — like an interest rate, e.g. 5–10%).
- **Right side** = (long-term reward of cooperating) ÷ (one-time gain from cheating).
- Why `r` is almost always smaller (so cooperation wins):
  - Top of the fraction = **$6B/year, every year, forever** → huge.
  - Bottom = a **one-shot** cheating bonus → small.
  - Big ÷ small = a very big number.
  - Your impatience `r` is just a few percent — tiny.
  - **Tiny ≤ Huge → inequality holds → cooperate.**
- **In one line:** *as long as the future stream is much bigger than the one-time temptation, no one is impatient enough to cheat.*

### How to walk the class through the two tables (30 seconds each)

**Game 1 — Smartphone competition (prisoner's dilemma):**
> "Read this table. Apple chooses *tolerate* or *sue*. Samsung chooses *differentiate* or *imitate*. Look at Samsung — *imitate* always pays better than *differentiate*. So Samsung copies. Apple sees that and *sues*. Both end up in the bottom-right cell — both lose legal fees. Both would prefer the top-left, but neither can trust the other to stay there. **That's a prisoner's dilemma.**"

**Game 2 — Supply chain (repeated game → Nash equilibrium):**
> "Same kind of table, different choices. Apple: *continue buying* or *diversify*. Samsung: *supply reliably* or *disrupt*. Look at the star cell — top-left — both make six billion dollars. Now ask: if Samsung disrupts, it gets one short-term gain but loses six billion *every year after*. If Apple diversifies, it pays huge transition costs and loses a key customer. **Neither side wants to move.** That's the **Nash equilibrium** — the spot where, given what the other does, no one can do better by deviating. Because the game repeats every year, cooperation locks in."

**One-line takeaway for the class:**
> *"In Game 1, defecting wins one round and ruins the relationship. In Game 2, cooperating wins every year — so they cooperate."*

**If asked: "Why does Apple sue? Low π still beats −Legal."**
> Tolerating *one round* is cheaper. But: (1) **Low π keeps decaying** toward zero as imitation continues (Bertrand). (2) Apple is also threatening **HTC, Motorola, Nokia, Amazon, Google** — tolerating Samsung kills every other threat. (3) The legal cost is **insurance** to keep future threats credible. Real comparison: *Low π → 0 across all rivals forever* vs *−Legal once, premium preserved.*

## Slide 11 — Replacement vs. Efficiency (Innovation Context)

**On screen:** Two boxes — *Replacement Effect* (Apple = entrant, nothing to lose) and *Efficiency Effect* (Samsung = incumbent, profits to protect). Maxim: *"The more you have to lose, the smaller your bets."*

**What to say:**
> "Step back from the lawsuit. Who actually innovates, and why? Two effects. The Replacement Effect — entrants make big disruptive bets because they have nothing to lose. Apple, with zero smartphone revenue before the iPhone, bet the company on a brand-new category. The Efficiency Effect — incumbents protect existing profits with smaller, incremental gains. Samsung's R&D climbs steadily — eight-point-three to nine-point-one billion dollars from 2010 to 2011. The maxim: the more you have to lose, the smaller your bets."

**Key terms:**
- **Replacement effect:** new entrants take big risks because their downside is small (no existing profits to cannibalize).
- **Efficiency effect:** incumbents prefer incremental upgrades — radical change risks cannibalizing their own profitable products.
- **Incumbent vs entrant:** *incumbent* = already in the market with profits. *Entrant* = newcomer.

Cannibalize means: one of a company’s own products takes sales away from another product of the same company.

## Slide 12 — Who Won (the conclusion)

**On screen:** Two scorecards (Apple's goals vs Samsung's goals — and outcomes). **Final damages: $539M** — only **8.4%** of Samsung's 2011 net income.

**What to say:**
> "Now we can answer the opening question. Apple's goals — damages, sales bans, slowing Android — mostly failed. Samsung's goals — number one in smartphones, keep the supply relationship, establish Android — all succeeded. The final damages, 539 million dollars, were just 8.4 percent of Samsung's 2011 net income. Painful — not existential.  "It hurt Samsung's wallet, not its life." The takeaway: when companies depend on each other, patents are bargaining chips, not weapons. Courts move slowly. Markets do not wait."

**Closing line — say it slowly:**
> *"Patents are bargaining chips, not weapons."*

> **Bargaining chip** = something you hold to *trade for what you want*, not to destroy the rival. Weapons kill; chips force a deal. Apple thought patents could kill Samsung — turns out they could only force a conversation.

## Slide 14 — Backup A2: Forward P/E (NEW — terminal data)

**On screen:** Two-line chart, monthly, Jan 2009 → Dec 2018. Apple (light blue) and Samsung (dark blue) **12-month forward P/E**. Four red dotted vertical markers at lawsuit dates. Faint dashed horizontal lines = each firm's median (Apple ≈13×, Samsung ≈8×). Below the chart: 4 small cards showing **Δ P/E (1mo before vs 1mo after)** at each event.

**What to say (only if asked — this is the reviewer's main ask):**
> "This backup chart answers the reviewer directly. Forward P/E for both firms, ten years, with the four lawsuit milestones marked. Apple traded at a structural premium — median around 13 times forward earnings, Samsung around 8 times. The deltas at each event tell the real story: the suit, the verdict, SCOTUS, the settlement — at every single one of them, the multiple changed by **one or less**. Investors did not re-rate either name on litigation news. The market priced this as a bargaining chip, not as existential."

**Key terms:**
- **Forward P/E (12M):** today's share price divided by *expected* earnings over the next 12 months. A "valuation multiple."
- **Re-rate:** when investors decide a company deserves a *different* multiple than before — usually because the long-term outlook changed.
- **Δ (delta):** here = average P/E in the month after the event minus average P/E in the month before. Tiny Δ → market shrugged.

**Source on slide:** Bloomberg consensus 12M forward P/E (AAPL, 005930.KS).

## Slide 16 — Backup A4: Aftermath

**On screen:** Three event cards — *2016 SCOTUS 8–0*, *2018 Retrial & Settlement ($539M)*, and *"And yet — the supply partnership continued."*

**What to say (only if asked):**
> "Two postscripts. In 2016, the U.S. Supreme Court ruled 8–0 that design-patent damages apply only to the *copied component* — for example the front face — not the whole phone. That narrowed the $399 million design-patent portion of the verdict. In 2018, a retrial set damages at $539 million, followed by a confidential settlement in June 2018 — ending seven years of litigation. **Key terms:**
- **SCOTUS 8–0:** Supreme Court of the United States, unanimous.
- **Design patent:** patent protecting the *look* of a product, not its function.
- **Retrial:** a fresh trial ordered to recompute damages after the 2016 ruling changed the rules.
